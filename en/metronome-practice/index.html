"I'm in time with the metronome on, but when I turn it off I speed up." It's the most common worry. Usually it isn't a lack of rhythm; it's having only practiced leaning on the click.

Why do we rush?

  • Rushing into hard spots: Right before a fill, a chord change or a fast passage, your body leans forward first.
  • Counting rests short: You can't wait through the silence and pull the next note early.
  • Nerves and excitement: When your heart rate climbs in rehearsal or on stage, the tempo climbs with it.
  • Only "following" the click: You can match it when it's there, but your ability to create the beat yourself is weak.

A 5-step metronome practice routine

Step 1. Start at a tempo where you make no mistakes

Start not at your target BPM but at a BPM you can play without a single mistake. Usually 60–70% of the target is right. Playing slow tempos accurately is the foundation of fast tempos.

Step 2. Raise it little by little after consistent success

Once you've played the same passage cleanly 3–4 times in a row, raise the BPM by only 2–5. Big jumps lock in mistakes. If you start making errors, it's fine to step back down.

Step 3. Hear the beat subdivided

With only a quarter-note click, the space between beats is empty and you drift. Turn on the 8th or 16th subdivision and listen with the gaps filled in. For songs in triplets, practice with the triplet subdivision.

Step 4. Reduce the click and create the beat yourself

Once you're comfortable, deliberately thin out the click. Let it sound only on 2 and 4, or just once per bar. Because you have to fill the empty beats yourself, your internal sense of time grows.

Step 5. Record and check

It may feel right while you play, but a recording tells a different story. Record even a short take, find where you pull ahead, and go back to step 1 for just that passage.

FAQ

How long should I use a metronome each day?

It's more effective to focus the click on passages you're learning and passages that feel shaky than to keep it on for your whole practice. Repeating short passages accurately improves you faster than running through the whole song with a click.

Can someone with "no rhythm" really improve?

Most of the time, they just never learned how to "feel" the beat. Slow tempos, listening to subdivisions and counting the beat with your body (tapping your foot, counting out loud) make a visible difference if you keep at it.

Should I listen through earphones?

With loud instruments like drums, you need earphones or headphones to hear the click accurately. To protect your hearing, raise the click volume only as much as you need.
