<h2>1. Start with a BPM for every song</h2>
<p>Most team tempo problems begin with deciding "what tempo are we doing this one?" by feel, every time. Before practice, write down these four things for each song.</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>BPM</strong> · Based on the original, adjusted to your team's level and the mood</li>
<li><strong>Time signature</strong> · 4/4, 6/8, 3/4 and so on</li>
<li><strong>Subdivision</strong> · Does it feel like 8ths, 16ths or a shuffle?</li>
<li><strong>Count-in</strong> · How many beats (or bars) before you come in</li>
</ul>
<p>For worship teams, simply noting this information whenever you receive the setlist cuts rehearsal time dramatically.</p>

<h2>2. Save songs in setlist order</h2>
<p>If you put songs in a list in the order of the show or service, you never have to re-enter BPMs when the song changes. When you play the same song next month, just load it again.</p>

<h2>3. Who hears the click, and how</h2>
<ul>
<li><strong>Only the drummer:</strong> The most common setup. If the drummer stays steady, the whole team follows.</li>
<li><strong>The whole team on in-ears:</strong> The most stable tempo, but check your monitor routing so the click never leaks into the house or congregation speakers.</li>
<li><strong>Only the leader:</strong> The worship leader or bandleader hears the click and leads the team with a count.</li>
</ul>

<h2>4. Smooth transitions between songs</h2>
<p>The shakiest moment is jumping straight into the next song's tempo after one ends. A spoken count-in for the next song lets everyone start on the same beat. If two songs with very different tempos follow each other, giving the count-in one extra bar also helps.</p>

<h1>What BPM means,<br><em>and a tempo marking chart.</em></h1>
<section class="story"><p class="eyebrow">WHY DAILYDRUM TEMPO</p><h2>That '♩=120' above the score is where today's practice starts.</h2><p>If you don't know the BPM, you set your practice speed by feel, and feel changes with your mood that day. <p class="lead">The big number on a metronome screen and the ♩= 120 above a score say the same thing. Once you understand BPM, you can set the practice speed for any song yourself.</p>

<h2>What is BPM?</h2>
<p><strong>BPM (Beats Per Minute)</strong> is the number of beats in one minute. BPM 60 is one beat per second; BPM 120 is two beats per second. The number on your metronome is this BPM, and the higher it is, the faster it goes.</p>
<p>If a score says <strong>♩ = 90</strong>, it means "a quarter note is one beat, 90 times per minute." Songs in 6/8 are sometimes marked with a dotted quarter, like ♩. = 60, so check which note value counts as one beat.</p>

<h2>Tempo marking BPM chart</h2>
<p>Tempo markings describe a feeling rather than an exact number, so ranges vary a little between sources. Below are commonly used approximate ranges.</p>
<table class="tbl">
<tr><th>Marking</th><th>Meaning</th><th>Approx. BPM</th></tr>
<tr><td>Largo</td><td>Very slow and broad</td><td>40–60</td></tr>
<tr><td>Larghetto</td><td>A little faster than largo</td><td>60–66</td></tr>
<tr><td>Adagio</td><td>Slow</td><td>66–76</td></tr>
<tr><td>Andante</td><td>At a walking pace</td><td>76–108</td></tr>
<tr><td>Moderato</td><td>Moderate</td><td>108–120</td></tr>
<tr><td>Allegro</td><td>Fast</td><td>120–156</td></tr>
<tr><td>Vivace</td><td>Lively and fast</td><td>156–176</td></tr>
<tr><td>Presto</td><td>Very fast</td><td>168–200</td></tr>
<tr><td>Prestissimo</td><td>As fast as possible</td><td>200+</td></tr>
</table>

<h2>Common BPM by genre</h2>
<p>Every song is different, but this helps you get a feel. <strong>Ballads</strong> often sit around 60–80, <strong>mid-tempo pop and worship</strong> 80–110, <strong>dance and up-tempo pop</strong> 110–130, <strong>rock</strong> 110–160, and <strong>punk and metal</strong> 160 and above. The best way to get the exact value is to measure it yourself while listening to the original.</p>

<h2>How to choose your practice BPM</h2>
<ol>
<li>Find the original BPM. If you don't know it, tap the screen along with the beat (TAP) while listening to get a rough BPM.</li>
<li>Check whether you can play it without mistakes at 60–70% of the original BPM.</li>
<li>When you play it cleanly several times in a row, raise it by 2–5 BPM.</li>
<li>Once you reach the original BPM, also try playing 5–10 faster. It gives you room to spare when nerves kick in on stage.</li>
</ol>

<p class="lead">The drummer is the band's clock. If the guitar wobbles a bit, the song goes on; if the drums wobble, everyone does. That's why a metronome should always be part of drum practice.</p>

<h2>First, make the click audible</h2>
<p>Drums are loud, and a click from a phone speaker gets buried fast.</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Use earphones or headphones:</strong> With isolating earphones or headphones the click is clear even at low volume. It's good hearing protection, too.</li>
<li><strong>Choose the sound:</strong> Pick a tone that doesn't disappear under cymbals and snare. Some people hear a sharp digital sound best; others prefer something softer.</li>
<li><strong>Accent the first beat:</strong> Give beat one a different sound or more volume so you never lose your place in the bar.</li>
</ul>

<h2>Step-by-step metronome practice for drums</h2>
<h3>1. Basic 8-beat groove</h3>
<p>Start at BPM 70–80 with 8th notes on the hi-hat, snare on 2 and 4, and kick on 1 and 3. If the click seems to "disappear" behind the hi-hat, you're right on it.</p>
<h3>2. Sharpen accuracy with 16th subdivisions</h3>
<p>For 16-beat grooves and fast kick patterns, practice with the 16th-note subdivision on. With the space between beats filled in, you hear right away where you're pulling ahead.</p>
<h3>3. Landing on beat one after a fill</h3>
<p>Fills are where drummers rush the most. Repeat "3 bars of groove + 1 bar of fill," and check that the crash and kick on the first beat after the fill land exactly with the click.</p>
<h3>4. Click only on 2 and 4</h3>
<p>Once you're comfortable, make the click sound only on beats 2 and 4. Playing the snare right on the click improves your backbeat feel, and you fill in 1 and 3 yourself.</p>
<h3>5. Shuffle and swing</h3>
<p>Practicing shuffle with a triplet subdivision click locks in the spacing between the first and third notes. For swing feel, practice with a drum machine whose swing amount you can adjust, and your ear picks it up quickly.</p>

<p class="eyebrow">PRACTICE GUIDE</p>
<h1>Practicing with a metronome,<br><em>for when you rush.</em></h1>
<section class="story"><p class="eyebrow">WHY DAILYDRUM TEMPO</p><h2>In time when you play alone, rushing as soon as the band comes in.</h2><p>With the click on, you're in time; with it off, you drift. It's not that you haven't practiced. It's that you've only practiced "following" the click. The ability to create the beat yourself only comes from changing how you practice.</p><p>DailyDrum, a one-on-one drum studio, has worked through this problem with many students whose timing wavered. We put that practice sequence into TEMPO so you can follow it on your own.</p><ul><li>Automator: from a BPM where you make no mistakes to your target BPM</li><li>Per-beat accent and mute so you fill the empty beats yourself</li><li>Per-song saving so yesterday's practice continues today</li></ul>{{BRAND}}</section>

<p class="lead">"I'm in time with the metronome on, but when I turn it off I speed up." It's the most common worry. Usually it isn't a lack of rhythm; it's having only practiced leaning on the click.</p>

<h2>Why do we rush?</h2>
<ul>
<li><strong>Rushing into hard spots:</strong> Right before a fill, a chord change or a fast passage, your body leans forward first.</li>
<li><strong>Counting rests short:</strong> You can't wait through the silence and pull the next note early.</li>
<li><strong>Nerves and excitement:</strong> When your heart rate climbs in rehearsal or on stage, the tempo climbs with it.</li>
<li><strong>Only "following" the click:</strong> You can match it when it's there, but your ability to create the beat yourself is weak.</li>
</ul>

<h2>A 5-step metronome practice routine</h2>
<h3>Step 1. Start at a tempo where you make no mistakes</h3>
<p>Start not at your target BPM but at <strong>a BPM you can play without a single mistake</strong>. Usually 60–70% of the target is right. Playing slow tempos accurately is the foundation of fast tempos.</p>
<h3>Step 2. Raise it little by little after consistent success</h3>
<p>Once you've played the same passage cleanly 3–4 times in a row, raise the BPM by only 2–5. Big jumps lock in mistakes. If you start making errors, it's fine to step back down.</p>
<h3>Step 3. Hear the beat subdivided</h3>
<p>With only a quarter-note click, the space between beats is empty and you drift. Turn on the 8th or 16th subdivision and listen with the gaps filled in. For songs in triplets, practice with the triplet subdivision.</p>
<h3>Step 4. Reduce the click and create the beat yourself</h3>
<p>Once you're comfortable, deliberately thin out the click. Let it sound only on 2 and 4, or just once per bar. Because you have to fill the empty beats yourself, your internal sense of time grows.</p>
<h3>Step 5. Record and check</h3>
<p>It may feel right while you play, but a recording tells a different story. Record even a short take, find where you pull ahead, and go back to step 1 for just that passage.</p>

<h2>FAQ</h2>
<h3>How long should I use a metronome each day?</h3>
<p>It's more effective to focus the click on <strong>passages you're learning and passages that feel shaky</strong> than to keep it on for your whole practice. Repeating short passages accurately improves you faster than running through the whole song with a click.</p>
<h3>Can someone with "no rhythm" really improve?</h3>
<p>Most of the time, they just never learned how to "feel" the beat. Slow tempos, listening to subdivisions and counting the beat with your body (tapping your foot, counting out loud) make a visible difference if you keep at it.</p>
<h3>Should I listen through earphones?</h3>
<p>With loud instruments like drums, you need earphones or headphones to hear the click accurately. To protect your hearing, raise the click volume only as much as you need.</p>
